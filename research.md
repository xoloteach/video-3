# Research and claim log — Video 3

**Topic:** choice overload, task switching, FoMO, and focus  
**Research date:** 2026-06-18

## Source log

1. **Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), _Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis_, Journal of Consumer Psychology.**  
   https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002  
   Meta-analysis of 99 observations (N=7,202). Supports: choice overload is conditional; larger assortments are more likely to hurt when the set is complex, the task is difficult, preferences are uncertain, or the chooser lacks a clear goal. Does **not** support the claim that every large choice set harms everyone.

2. **Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), _Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload_, Journal of Consumer Research.**  
   https://doi.org/10.1086/651235  
   Meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N=5,036). The mean effect was near zero with substantial variation. This is the key counterweight: “choice overload” is not a universal law and the script should frame it as something that **can** make choice harder.

3. **Monsell (2003), _Task switching_, Trends in Cognitive Sciences.**  
   https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7  
   Review supports: switching tasks has transient and longer-term performance costs; preparation reduces but does not eliminate costs. It supports the script’s qualitative “there is a cost” language, not a universal quantified productivity loss.

4. **Przybylski et al. (2013), _Motivational, emotional, and behavioral correlates of fear of missing out_, Computers in Human Behavior.**  
   https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.02.014  
   Supports the established FoMO construct and associations with social-media engagement in the study sample. Associations do not establish that social media directly causes FoMO.

5. **Tandon et al. (2021), _Fear of missing out (FoMO) among social media users: a systematic literature review_, Internet Research.**  
   https://doi.org/10.1108/INTR-11-2019-0455  
   Systematic review of 58 empirical studies. Supports discussing FoMO as a researched construct with diverse definitions and outcomes; it does not justify clinical or causal claims.

## Script validation

- **Retained:** “choice overload” is presented as a phenomenon that *can* make decisions harder, matching the mixed evidence.
- **Retained:** frequent task switches have a cost, phrased qualitatively and consistent with task-switching research.
- **Retained with limitation:** FoMO is described as fear around passing up alternatives; the narration does not claim it is a disorder or that it affects all internet users.
- **Practical advice:** constraints, schedules, priorities, and committing to a decision are framed as optional behavioural tools—not medical treatment or guaranteed outcomes.
- **Not claimed:** that 100 options literally destroys focus for everyone, that Netflix causes a measurable outcome, or that all successful people persist in one path.

## Competitive-angle note

The common “paradox of choice” format centres on shopping. This video’s distinct spine is that abundance creates an **attention-management problem**: keeping every future open can prevent present commitment. The title is rhetorical; the voiceover supplies the scientific limitation.
